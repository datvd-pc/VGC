# AI As A Work Layer, Not A Toy

AI is useful when it reduces ambiguity.
It is not useful when it only makes work look faster.

That distinction matters a lot for game teams.

The most practical use of AI is not to replace thinking.
It is to support thinking.

The formula is:

> AI layer = input cleaner + thought organizer + memory assistant

Where can it help?

- Meeting agenda;
- Meeting summary;
- Brief scan;
- Question generation;
- Compare options;
- Learning plan;
- Flashcards;
- Risk lists;
- First drafts of emails or recaps.

Where should it not be used blindly?

- Design judgment;
- Final business decision;
- Product taste;
- Anything that needs direct ownership.

For a young game Designer, AI is especially useful for:

- Comparing mechanics;
- Reviewing level notes;
- Summarizing player feedback;
- Spotting patterns in fail Data;
- Learning the language of design faster.

For a PM, AI is useful for:

- Preparing before a meeting;
- Keeping a better decision trail;
- Turning noise into a list;
- Building a clearer weekly review.

For a Founder, AI is useful for:

- Scenario comparison;
- Risk notes;
- Deal preparation;
- Decision reflection.

The mistake is to use AI as a decoration.
That creates output, but not better judgment.

If I were a junior, I would use AI as a tutor and a mirror.
If I were a lead, I would use AI to reduce the overhead around decision-making.

In the next 2-4 years, people who use AI as a work layer will move much faster than people who use it as a writing toy.

AI should reduce ambiguity.
Not decorate it.

The practical test is simple:

> Did AI help you make a better decision, or did it only help you create more text?

If the answer is only more text, you are probably using it wrong.

For game teams, AI should sit inside the daily workflow.

Before a meeting:

- Scan the brief;
- List unclear assumptions;
- Prepare questions;
- Identify risks;
- Draft the decision needed.

During production:

- Turn feature notes into tasks;
- Check whether scope matches the test goal;
- Create QA edge cases;
- Compare implementation options;
- Summarize what changed between builds.

After a test:

- Organize Data observations;
- Separate facts from guesses;
- Draft a postmortem;
- List next hypotheses;
- Prepare a short Publisher update.

For a Junior Game Designer, I would build an AI routine like this:

1. Write the level hypothesis in one sentence.
2. Ask AI to challenge the hypothesis from player behavior.
3. Check the level against clarity, fairness, near-miss, and retry motivation.
4. After Data arrives, ask AI to help organize patterns.
5. Rewrite the lesson in my own words.

That last step matters.

If you cannot rewrite the lesson yourself, you probably did not learn it.

For a PM, I would build a different routine:

1. Ask AI to turn rough meeting notes into decisions, owners, and risks.
2. Remove anything AI invented.
3. Add missing context from the team.
4. Send a short recap.
5. Save the recap into the decision log.

For a Founder, I would use AI to compare scenarios:

- Keep current Cashflow model;
- Invest in Data capability;
- Hire a Senior PM;
- Pitch Publishers;
- Build one original IP;
- Reduce team size and extend runway.

AI will not decide for you.
But it can force you to see tradeoffs more clearly.

The risk is that AI makes weak thinking look fluent.

That will become a real problem.

Many people will write better updates, better pitch decks, and better strategy notes. But if the underlying thinking is shallow, experienced Publishers and managers will still see it.

The future advantage will not belong to people who “use AI.”
Almost everyone will use AI.

The advantage will belong to people who use AI to build better judgment, cleaner systems, and faster learning loops.

That means AI should sit in specific moments of the work, not as a vague promise.

Before work:

- Clarify the goal;
- Gather rough inputs;
- Ask AI to surface missing questions;
- Draft a first structure.

During work:

- Compare options;
- Summarize complex notes;
- Clean up wording;
- Generate edge cases;
- Turn weak thinking into visible tradeoffs.

After work:

- Check what AI got wrong;
- Keep the corrected version;
- Store the lesson;
- Reuse the pattern next time.

That loop turns AI into operating leverage.

For game teams in Vietnam, this is a real chance.

Many teams do not need AI to “do the job.”
They need AI to reduce the time spent on low-value thinking and repetitive cleanup.

That is where the gain is.
