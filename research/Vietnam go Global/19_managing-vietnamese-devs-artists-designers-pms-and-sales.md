# Managing Vietnamese Devs, Artists, Designers, PMs And Sales

One management style does not fit every role.

That sounds obvious. In practice, many teams still manage people as if the role does not matter.

In Vietnam game Studios, each role tends to have its own strengths and its own failure mode.

### Dev
Strength: execution speed, technical adaptation, build discipline.  
Risk: can under-communicate issues until too late.

### Artist
Strength: strong production, visual output, flexibility.  
Risk: may need very clear direction and review criteria.

### Designer
Strength: mechanic thinking, game feel, fast iteration.  
Risk: may confuse novelty with quality or difficulty with depth.

### PM / Producer
Strength: coordination, moving work forward, keeping things alive.  
Risk: may become a task manager instead of an outcome owner.

### Sales / BD
Strength: energy, relationship, deal making.  
Risk: may overpromise if scope and control are not clear.

The formula is:

> Role management = role-specific output + role-specific risk signal

That means a good manager does not give the same feedback style to everyone.

To a dev, you may need to talk about bug risk, build flow, performance, and release stability.
To a Designer, you may need to talk about fairness, trust, economy, and player memory.
To a PM, you may need to talk about decision ownership and escalation.
To sales, you may need to talk about promise control and scope control.

That is a practical operating skill, not a soft one.

If I were a Publisher, I would want to know which role on the team actually owns which decision.
If I were a Founder, I would want each role to have a clear output and a clear risk signal.

In the next 2-4 years, teams that can manage roles well will move faster and burn less.
Teams that manage everyone the same way will keep confusing activity with progress.

Different roles.
Different strengths.
Different risks.
Different management.

This also means you should not evaluate people only by how busy they are.

A busy dev is not always a good dev.
A busy PM is not always a good PM.
A busy sales person is not always a safe sales person.

The better question is:

- What kind of risk does this role create if unmanaged?
- What kind of output does this role create when managed well?

That question is much more useful.

For example:

- Devs need clean requirements and stable build flow.
- Artists need strong direction and clear review criteria.
- Designers need Data and trust signals.
- PMs need decision ownership and escalation rules.
- Sales need promise control and written scope.

If you manage all of them the same way, you are not leading.
You are just applying noise uniformly.

If I were a Founder, I would build role-specific checklists.
If I were a Publisher, I would want to know which role owns what before I trust the team with a larger scope.

That is the real management work.

The practical way to manage Vietnamese talent is to recognize the incentive shape of each role.

Devs often want:

- Stable scope;
- Technical challenge;
- Respect for their time;
- Enough detail to build correctly;
- Fewer late changes.

Artists often want:

- Clear direction;
- Fast feedback;
- Visual standards;
- Less vague criticism;
- Recognition of craft.

Designers often want:

- Room to think;
- Data access;
- Honest review;
- The chance to improve the core loop;
- Less “just make it fun” advice.

PMs often want:

- Decision clarity;
- Escalation support;
- Less hidden conflict;
- Fewer surprise changes;
- Help with alignment.

Sales or business roles often want:

- A clear promise;
- A stable deliverable;
- Realistic scope;
- A strong delivery team;
- Fewer internal contradictions.

When you understand the incentive, you can manage the risk.

That does not mean everybody gets everything they want.

It means you stop giving generic management to different problems.

In a Vietnam Studio, that can be the difference between friction and flow.

The easiest way to start is to ask each role one question:

- What do you need to do your best work?
- What usually blocks you?
- What should the team stop doing to help you?

Those answers are more useful than generic motivation speeches.

They show you where the real management work is.

Once you know the answers, you can adjust the system instead of blaming the people.
That is what real management looks like.
