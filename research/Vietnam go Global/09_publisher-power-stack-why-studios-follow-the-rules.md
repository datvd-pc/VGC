# Publisher Power Stack: Why Studios Follow The Rules

Studio talent is real. Publisher power is still bigger.

That is not a moral statement. It is a structural one.

A strong Publisher usually controls five layers that matter in Puzzle and Hybrid Puzzle:

1. Capital
2. Big Data
3. Legal leverage
4. Distribution
5. Operating System

Capital pays for UA, milestone support, burn rate, and soft-launch risk.
Big Data gives the Publisher a better read on creative, cohort quality, and game performance across many titles.
Legal leverage decides who owns the IP, source code, test Data, exclusivity, sequel rights, and publishing rights.
Distribution decides who can actually scale the game.
Operating System decides whether the Publisher can make the game better after launch.

That is why Studios follow Publisher rules.

They do not only follow the money. They follow access.

If you are a Studio, this matters because a Revenue Share deal can feel fair on paper and still leave you with very little power in practice. If the Publisher controls Data, rights, and scale decisions, the Studio may win cash but lose learning.

That is the hidden cost.

The formula is:

> Publisher power = capital + Big Data + legal + distribution + Operating System

Once you see the stack, you can see why so many Studios become dependent. It is not just because they need money. It is because the Publisher often controls the path to scale and the records of what happened along the way.

This is also why deal structure matters more than many Founders think.

The important questions are not only:

- How much Revenue Share do we get?
- What is the advance?
- What is the UA budget?

You also need to ask:

- Who owns the Data?
- Who owns the source?
- Who owns the creative learning?
- Who decides scale or kill?
- What happens if the game underperforms?
- What learning does the Studio retain?

This is the difference between selling output and selling capability.

If a Studio is only selling output, the Publisher can replace it.
If a Studio is selling capability, the Publisher has a reason to stay.

That is why the best Studios should not just negotiate revenue. They should negotiate learning rights.

In the next 2-4 years, this will matter even more. AI will make the supply of prototypes larger. That means Publishers will have more choice, and Studios will face more competition. In that environment, the Studio that keeps learning will have a real advantage.

If I were a Studio, I would try to keep:

- Access to dashboards;
- Postmortem rights;
- Reusable insights;
- Sequel rights where possible;
- Clarity on IP and source ownership;
- A written record of what was learned.

If I were a Publisher, I would not treat every Studio the same. A vendor needs one deal structure. A prototype hunter needs another. An Operator-builder needs a third.

That is the point most people miss:

> The same contract can either build a partner or keep a supplier.

This is not theory. It is leverage.

If you work with Vietnam Studios and you do not understand the power stack, you will misread the deal.

The power stack also explains why some Studios feel “dependent” even when they are technically capable.

The Studio may be good at:

- Art;
- Code;
- Design;
- Speed;
- Iteration.

But if the Publisher owns the Data, the rights, the distribution access, and the decision flow, then the Studio is not really standing on equal ground.

That is why money alone can mislead people.

A Studio can earn enough to feel strong and still have little real leverage.

If you want to see whether a deal is healthy, ask what the Studio keeps after the launch:

- Does it keep the learning?
- Does it keep access?
- Does it keep reusable insight?
- Does it keep the right to build the next thing better?

Those are leverage questions.

They matter because the next deal usually starts where the last one ended.
If the Studio learned nothing and kept nothing, it starts from zero again.

That is expensive.

For Vietnamese Founders, this is a particularly important lesson.
It is easy to be grateful for opportunity. It is harder to be disciplined about control.
But in this business, control is what allows you to compound.

The Publisher stack is not something to resent.
It is something to understand so that you can negotiate better and build better.

If you understand the stack, you can stop mistaking access for ownership.

That is a real maturity jump.

The practical lesson is simple.

If you are a Studio, do not ask only for more work.
Ask what kind of power you are being invited into.

Are you being given:

- Execution power;
- Learning power;
- Product influence;
- IP participation;
- Or only temporary production access?

Those are very different roles.

A Studio that understands the Power Stack can negotiate better because it knows what matters most in each deal.

Sometimes the answer is not more percentage.
Sometimes the answer is more visibility.
Sometimes the answer is reuse rights.
Sometimes the answer is a clearer next-project path.
Sometimes the answer is “this deal is fine, but it does not move us forward.”

That last answer is important.

Many young teams confuse any opportunity with a good opportunity.

The market rewards the team that can say no to the wrong kind of yes.

For a foreign Publisher, this is also useful.

If you want better Vietnamese partners, do not treat every relationship as a one-way hierarchy. The moment you transfer enough learning for the Studio to become stronger, the partnership gets healthier.

Healthy partnerships are easier to repeat.

Repeatable partnerships are where the real value sits.

The practical next step is to negotiate for what the stack usually hides:

- Visibility into Data;
- Clarity on rights;
- Access to learning;
- Protection of reusable work;
- A path to the next project.

That is where the Studio starts to gain real leverage.
