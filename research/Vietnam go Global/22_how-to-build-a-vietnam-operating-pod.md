# How To Build A Vietnam Operating Pod

You do not need a perfect Vietnamese Studio.
You need the right operating pod.

That is a much better way to think about early collaboration.

A lot of foreign teams imagine the whole Studio as one thing. In practice, a good pod is more useful than a big abstract promise.

What should the pod contain?

- One local PM;
- One senior dev lead;
- One game Designer or economy thinker;
- One analytics layer, internal or external;
- One creative bridge;
- One weekly decision cadence.

That is enough to start.

The formula is:

> Operating pod = local PM + senior dev lead + Designer/economy + analytics + creative bridge + weekly cadence

Why does this work?

Because it gives the Publisher clarity without destroying local speed.
It also gives the Vietnamese team a clear structure for responsibility.

The common mistake is to ask for a huge partnership when the team is still operating in a much smaller reality.
The pod is a way to test seriousness without pretending the system is bigger than it is.

If I were a Publisher, I would begin with a 6-8 week pod, not a vague long-term promise.
If I were a Studio, I would rather join a clear pod than a blurry “let’s keep talking” relationship.

This is especially useful in Vietnam because many teams are strong at production but still developing the operating muscle for Live Ops, Data cadence, and structured communication. A pod makes those gaps visible fast.

In the next 2-4 years, I expect more serious Publishers to use pod structures in Vietnam instead of asking every partner to behave like a full Operator on day one.

The point is not to make the team smaller.
The point is to make the collaboration clearer.

The Operating Pod model is useful because it matches the real situation of many Vietnamese game teams.

They are often not ready to become full global Operators on day one. But they can become very strong compact teams if the working system is clear.

That difference matters.

If a Publisher expects a young Studio to suddenly behave like a mature global Live Ops organization, disappointment is almost guaranteed. If a Studio expects a Publisher to transfer all operating knowledge without a structured process, that is also unrealistic.

The pod is the middle path.

It creates a small unit where both sides can see behavior clearly.

A practical 6-8 week pod should answer these questions:

- Can the team understand the player promise?
- Can the team turn a brief into a focused playable build?
- Can the team cut scope intelligently?
- Can the team set up basic events?
- Can the team explain risk before it becomes expensive?
- Can the team write clear updates in English?
- Can the team learn from one test and improve the next one?

This is more useful than asking whether the Studio is “good” in general.

Good at what?

Good at production?
Good at design?
Good at Data?
Good at communication?
Good at Live Ops?
Good at working with Western Partners?

The pod makes those answers visible.

The weekly cadence should be light but strict.

Monday:
Agree the hypothesis, scope, risk, owner, and decision needed.

Wednesday:
Review playable progress, blockers, assumptions, and unclear decisions.

Friday:
Lock what changed, what was learned, and what must be prepared for the next gate.

After every test:
Write a short postmortem with metric result, likely reason, confidence level, and next action.

This is not corporate theater.

It is the minimum structure needed for speed to become learning.

AI should also sit inside the pod as a work layer.

Good use of AI:

- Summarize meetings into owners and next steps;
- Translate unclear feedback into decision questions;
- Compare competitor loops;
- Draft risk logs;
- Create QA checklists;
- Produce first-pass economy assumptions;
- Clean up English updates;
- Prepare postmortem drafts.

Bad use of AI:

- Replacing product judgment;
- Generating generic game ideas;
- Writing long reports nobody reads;
- Hiding unclear thinking behind polished language.

The pod should also define what each role must own.

The Dev Lead owns technical risk and implementation cost.
The Game Designer owns player behavior and feature priority.
The Artist or Creative Lead owns readability and market-facing visual promise.
The Local PM owns clarity, cadence, documentation, and escalation.
The Publisher PM owns direction, test logic, and feedback quality.

If any of these are unclear, the pod becomes a task channel instead of an operating unit.

My prediction:

In the next 2-4 years, foreign Publishers who work well in Vietnam will not only search for cheap production. They will build repeatable pods with clear operating rules.

Vietnam can offer speed.
But speed becomes valuable only when it is connected to Data, communication, and decision discipline.

The pod is also a good way to test whether the team is ready for more responsibility.

If the pod can stay clear for one cycle, it can often earn a bigger one.
If it cannot stay clear, the problem is usually not talent. It is operating shape.

That is a useful diagnosis for both sides.

If I wanted to make the visual concrete, I would show:

- 6 roles;
- 3 operating habits;
- 6-8 weeks;
- 1 decision log;
- 1 postmortem;
- 1 trust score after the cycle.

That gives the pod a shape people can immediately understand.
