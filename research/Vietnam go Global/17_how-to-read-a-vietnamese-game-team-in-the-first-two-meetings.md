# How To Read A Vietnamese Game Team In The First Two Meetings

The first two meetings tell you more than most people think.

They will not tell you everything. But they are enough to read whether a Vietnamese team is selling output, speed, or operating capability.

That matters because the wrong read creates the wrong deal.

A good team in Vietnam can look different from a mature global Operator. A team may be fast, polite, responsive, and technically strong, but still not ready for a deeper partnership. Another team may look rough on the surface but already think in systems, learning, and ownership.

The difference is not cosmetic. It is structural.

What should you look for in the first two meetings?

Start with the questions that reveal how the team thinks:

- What Cashflow path are you on today?
- What was the last project that failed, and what did you learn?
- What does your team keep after each prototype?
- Who owns Data, decision, and follow-up?
- What happens when a project looks weak?
- How do you decide kill, iterate, or scale?

Those answers tell you more than a polished deck.

The formula is:

> First two meetings = Cashflow path + learning path + control path

If the team only talks about what they built, you are reading output.
If the team talks about what they learned, you are reading maturity.
If the team talks about how they operate when things get messy, you are reading potential.

This is especially important in Vietnam because many Studios are still young and many Founders are still in the early stages of global Operating Maturity. That is normal. But it means a foreign partner should not confuse warmth, speed, and responsiveness with partnership readiness.

If I were a Publisher, I would leave the first meeting with three notes:

1. How they make money today.
2. How they learn from failure.
3. How much control they are ready to share.

If I were a Vietnamese Studio, I would prepare those three answers before meeting any serious foreign partner.

In the next 2-4 years, the teams that can answer those questions clearly will get more trust, better deals, and faster decisions.

The first two meetings are not about charm.
They are about risk reading.

There is a second reason the first two meetings matter.

They show whether the team is likely to become easier or harder to work with later.

Some signs are good:

- The team can answer directly;
- The team can name what it does not know;
- The team can explain what happened in a failed project;
- The team can talk about process without sounding defensive;
- The team can say no when necessary.

Some signs are risky:

- Every answer sounds polished but vague;
- No one can name the owner;
- No one can explain the last failure clearly;
- No one can separate wish from evidence;
- The team says yes to everything too quickly.

The foreign partner is not just buying a project.
They are buying a working relationship.

That means the meeting is not only about the prototype. It is also about the way the team thinks under pressure.

If I were a Publisher, I would leave with a simple judgment:

- Is this team easy to read?
- Is this team easy to trust?
- Is this team easy to steer?

Those are often better questions than “Is this prototype cool?”

Cool prototypes do not always become good partnerships.
Good partnerships usually start with readable teams.

If I were the foreign partner, I would also watch for three small but important signs.

First: how the team handles uncertainty.

Do they ask clear questions, or do they guess quietly?

Second: how the team handles correction.

Do they get defensive, or do they adjust quickly?

Third: how the team handles ownership.

Do they know who makes the call, or does everyone wait for someone else?

These signs are more useful than one enthusiastic pitch.

In the first two meetings, I would want to see:

- Who speaks for the team;
- Who writes notes;
- Who asks the hard questions;
- Who can explain the product logic;
- Who can talk about risk without panic.

If the team can do that, I am more interested.

If not, I know the deal needs more structure.

This is also a useful self-test for Vietnamese Founders.

If your own team is hard to read internally, a foreign partner will feel that too.

The first two meetings are not only for selling.
They are for revealing how the team works.

That is why I would judge less by polish and more by readability.

Readable teams are easier to trust, easier to steer, and easier to work with over time.
That is usually the better bet.
