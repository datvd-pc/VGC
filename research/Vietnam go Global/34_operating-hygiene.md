# Operating Hygiene

A strong team is not the team that does the most.
It is the team that remembers the best.

That is why operating hygiene matters.

In game teams, work gets lost all the time:

- Notes disappear;
- Decisions are remembered differently;
- Risks are not written down;
- Handoffs get messy;
- Postmortems never happen;
- Weekly reviews become random.

The formula is:

> Operating hygiene = memory + cadence + accountability

The minimum set is simple:

- Notes;
- Decision log;
- Risk log;
- Postmortem;
- Handoff;
- Weekly review.

Each item has a purpose.

- Notes keep the trace.
- Decision logs explain why choices were made.
- Risk logs show what is still uncertain.
- Postmortems stop repeat mistakes.
- Handoffs prevent work from falling through cracks.
- Weekly reviews keep the team learning.

AI and tools can help a lot here.

- AI can summarize meetings.
- AI can draft postmortems.
- Notion or Docs can keep the templates.
- A shared folder can keep the memory.

This seems small.
It is not.

Weak operating hygiene becomes expensive when the team gets bigger.
At that point, the team is not just losing time. It is losing memory.

If I were a Founder, I would make sure decision log and postmortem are not optional.
If I were a PM, I would treat weekly review as part of production, not as extra work.

In the next 2-4 years, the teams with better memory will learn faster than the teams with more headcount.

Workflow is memory in action.

Operating Hygiene means the team has fewer hidden problems.

In many Vietnamese Studios, the team is small, fast, and informal. That can be a strength. People talk quickly. Decisions are made quickly. A Developer can sit next to a Designer and fix something immediately.

But when the team works with a foreign Publisher, the informal style can break.

The Publisher cannot see the room.
The Publisher cannot read local context.
The Publisher may not know that a feature is risky until the build is late.
The Studio may think a decision was obvious because everyone local understood it.

That is where Operating Hygiene matters.

The basics:

- One task board;
- One decision log;
- One risk log;
- One source of truth for scope;
- One event map;
- One build checklist;
- One postmortem format;
- One owner for partner communication.

This does not need to be heavy.

A small Studio can run this with Notion, Google Docs, Linear, Jira, Trello, Slack, Discord, or any practical tool. The tool is less important than the habit.

The habit is:

> Important information should not live only in someone’s head.

AI can reduce the cost of this habit.

Use it to:

- Clean meeting notes;
- Format risk logs;
- Compare current scope with original brief;
- Generate QA checklist drafts;
- Summarize weekly progress;
- Turn messy feedback into categories;
- Prepare postmortem structure.

But do not outsource ownership.

Someone still needs to check whether the note is true.
Someone still needs to decide what matters.
Someone still needs to follow up when risk appears.

My recommended minimum system for a young Vietnam Studio:

**Daily**

- Update blockers;
- Flag scope changes;
- Record major decisions.

**Weekly**

- Review milestone;
- Review risks;
- Review Data or playtest findings;
- Confirm next decision.

**After every build**

- Record what changed;
- Record what broke;
- Record what was learned;
- Record what should be reused.

This is how a team builds memory.

Without memory, every project becomes a fresh struggle.

In the next 2-4 years, Operating Hygiene will become a bigger advantage because production speed will keep increasing. AI, better tools, and more templates will make building easier. But faster building also creates faster confusion if the Operating System is weak.

The best small teams will not be the most formal.

They will be the ones with just enough structure to keep learning visible.

That structure also needs one owner.

If nobody owns the Operating System, the Operating System slowly disappears.

I would assign one person to each:

- Decision log;
- Risk log;
- Meeting recap;
- Postmortem template;
- Weekly review.

It does not need to be a full-time role.
It needs to be somebody’s job.

That one detail matters because “everyone” often means “no one.”

When the team gets busy, hygiene is the first thing to vanish.
That is exactly when it matters most.

If I were a Founder, I would ask monthly:

> Can we still explain what happened last week without asking three people separately?

If the answer is no, the system is too weak.
