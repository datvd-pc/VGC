# Deal Flow Architecture Is The First Moat

Stay Hungry. Don't Be Foolish.

The question is not:

"Where can we find more Vietnamese Studios?"

The better question is:

"How do we design sourcing, qualification, prioritization, and conversion so the best Studios do not disappear inside a noisy pipeline?"

Many foreign Publishers look at Vietnam and see a simple sourcing question.

They ask who knows the good teams.

They ask which events to attend.

They ask which city has the strongest talent.

They ask whether the best Studios are in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang, or hidden inside small private networks.

Those are useful questions.

But they are not enough.

Vietnam is not only a contact-list problem.

Vietnam is a Deal Flow architecture problem.

You can meet many Studios and still fail if you do not know how to qualify them.

You can receive many prototypes and still fail if your review process is slow.

You can attend many events and still fail if follow-up is weak.

You can know the market and still lose the best teams if your first interaction feels unclear.

Deal Flow is not luck.

It is infrastructure.

## Past

In an easier market, a Publisher could operate with a looser pipeline.

Good referrals mattered.

Event conversations mattered.

Cold messages sometimes worked.

A small group of scouts, PMs, or BD people could manually remember which team was interesting, which prototype had promise, and which Founder seemed reliable.

That was messy, but manageable.

It also fit an earlier stage of the Vietnamese market, when many Studios were still small, private, and relationship-driven.

The Publisher could rely on personal networks.

The Studio could rely on introductions.

The process did not need to be perfect because the volume was lower and expectations were simpler.

## Present

That is changing.

Vietnam has strong game output, but Studio maturity is uneven.

Some teams can move fast but lack analytics.

Some teams can produce polish but lack Monetization thinking.

Some teams can prototype quickly but do not communicate risk clearly.

Some teams have Cashflow but no clear global ambition.

Some teams are strong but quiet, private, and careful about who they trust.

This creates a problem for foreign Publishers.

More contact does not automatically create better opportunities.

It can create more noise.

The stronger question is:

Can the Publisher build a funnel that separates weak signal from strong signal early?

**Deal Flow Architecture.**

That is the keyword.

The basic funnel has four stages:

1. Source.
2. Qualify.
3. Prioritize.
4. Convert.

Each stage needs rules.

Without rules, the pipeline becomes emotional.

The team spends too much time on polite conversations.

Strong Studios wait too long for clear answers.

Weak leads consume senior attention.

Good opportunities are lost because nobody owns the next step.

## Evidence

The logic is visible in Vietnam's e-commerce and startup ecosystems.

E-commerce platforms do not only collect sellers.

They acquire sellers, qualify them, onboard them, monitor performance, solve disputes, and make repeat transactions easier.

Startup ecosystems do not only collect Founders.

They improve pipeline quality through events, mentoring, early-stage finance, screening, and follow-on support.

Publishing should work the same way.

A Studio is not just a contact.

A Studio is a potential Portfolio asset.

That means the Publisher needs a way to evaluate:

- Team maturity;
- Genre fit;
- Playable quality;
- Data readiness;
- Communication reliability;
- Iteration speed;
- Monetization understanding;
- Trust risk.

If those criteria stay only inside one scout's head, the Publisher does not have a system.

It has memory risk.

## Future

Over the next 2-4 years, AI will increase the number of prototypes, creatives, and game variants in the market.

That will not make Deal Flow easier.

It will make filtering more important.

The best Publishers will not only ask for more submissions.

They will build better intake.

They will define what "qualified" means.

They will respond faster.

They will reject earlier.

They will nurture selectively.

They will track which source channels produce repeat partners, not only first conversations.

This matters in Vietnam because many good teams are still learning how to present themselves to global partners.

A weak deck does not always mean a weak team.

A confident pitch does not always mean a strong team.

The Publisher needs an evaluation system that can see beyond surface signals.

I would also expect the sourcing channels to change.

In the old model, a Publisher could depend heavily on personal networks, known Founders, and event introductions.

In the next model, the stronger Publisher will combine multiple channels:

- Direct Founder referrals;
- Event-qualified leads;
- Game jam and prototype signals;
- LinkedIn and community observation;
- Portfolio company referrals;
- Recruiter or HR market intelligence;
- Local advisor screening;
- Public launch and download tracking;
- Tool or platform partner Data.

Each channel has a different bias.

Founder referrals may be high trust but narrow.

Events may produce volume but mixed quality.

Public launch tracking may reveal output but not team maturity.

Local advisors may reveal hidden teams but need clear criteria.

That is why sourcing cannot stand alone.

It must connect to qualification.

## For Publishers

Your first moat is not capital.

Your first moat is signal quality.

If your BD process cannot tell the difference between a strong but quiet Studio and a weak but confident Studio, you will misallocate attention.

If your PM review process has no SLA, you will lose trust before the first deal.

If your qualification criteria are unclear, the Studio will not know what to improve.

The minimum system should include:

- Intake form;
- Playable review checklist;
- Studio maturity score;
- Response SLA;
- Pilot decision rule;
- Owner for follow-up;
- Reason code for rejection or nurture.

This does not need to be complex.

It needs to be explicit.

If I were a Publisher PM building this system, I would start with one simple rule:

No lead moves forward without a written reason.

Not a feeling.

Not "looks good."

Not "Founder seems nice."

A written reason.

For example:

- Strong Puzzle craft but weak Data;
- Strong production team, needs Monetization support;
- Good Founder learning speed, early build not ready;
- Fast prototype team, unclear Retention thinking;
- High polish, low strategic fit.

This forces the organization to build memory.

It also makes the next conversation more useful.

## For Studios

If you want to be found, reduce evaluation friction.

Do not only send a build.

Send context.

Send:

- What the game is;
- What player behavior you expect;
- What has been tested;
- What the numbers say;
- What you need from the Publisher;
- What you can deliver in the next 2-4 weeks;
- What you do not yet know.

This makes you easier to qualify.

It also shows maturity.

In a noisy market, clarity is a competitive advantage.

Studios should also understand that a Publisher pipeline is not a moral judgment.

Being rejected quickly can be better than waiting politely for six weeks.

Being placed in Nurture can be better than forcing a weak pilot.

Being asked for clearer Data is not an insult.

It is the Publisher testing whether the Studio can become readable.

If you are a Studio, your job is to reduce ambiguity.

The faster a Publisher can understand you, the more fairly it can allocate attention.

## For Investors And Operators

Do not measure Vietnam pipeline only by number of leads.

Measure:

- Qualified lead rate;
- Time to first response;
- Time to pilot decision;
- Source quality;
- Repeat partner rate;
- Learning captured per rejected lead.

Rejected leads are not always waste.

If the Publisher learns why a category of Studio is not ready, the system gets smarter.

If the Publisher keeps forgetting the same lesson, the system is not learning.

The operating danger is false volume.

False volume means the team has many leads but few qualified options.

It feels healthy from the outside.

Inside, it creates fatigue.

Senior reviewers get tired.

Good Studios wait longer.

Weak leads receive vague encouragement.

The team starts judging the whole market as low quality.

But the real issue may be pipeline design.

A clean pipeline should reduce emotional noise.

It should make the team calmer, faster, and more consistent.

## 7-Day Action

Create a one-page Studio intake scorecard.

Use eight fields:

1. Genre fit.
2. Team maturity.
3. Playable quality.
4. Data readiness.
5. Communication reliability.
6. Iteration speed.
7. Monetization understanding.
8. Trust risk.

Then define three SLAs:

- First response time;
- Review time;
- Pilot decision time.

The SLA matters because trust starts before the first deal.
