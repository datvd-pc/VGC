# Deal Structure Decides Learning

Most people think a good deal is about revenue split.
For Vietnamese Studios, that is only the surface.

The deeper question is what the deal teaches the team after the launch.

A Studio can earn money from a strong Rev-Share deal and still come out weaker if it loses access to the Data, the source, the post-launch learning, or the right to reuse what it learned later.

That is why deal structure is not just commercial.
It is educational.

The formula is:

> Deal = money + learning + control + exit rules

If any of those four are unclear, the deal is incomplete.

For a Vietnamese Studio, the most important questions are:

- Who owns the IP?
- Who owns the source?
- Who owns the Data?
- Who sees the dashboards?
- Who decides scale or kill?
- What happens if the game underperforms?
- What can be reused in the next project?

Those questions are not aggressive.
They are professional.

If you are a Publisher, the deal should match the Studio’s maturity.

- A vendor needs tighter scope and tighter control.
- A prototype hunter needs fast test logic.
- A reskin Studio needs Cashflow clarity.
- An emerging Operator needs learning rights.
- A mature Operator needs strategic alignment.

If you are a Studio, you should stop asking for “better terms” before you can show better discipline.

The next 2-4 years will reward teams that can show they actually learn from every project. A clean deck is not enough. A nice prototype is not enough. A good pitch is not enough. The stronger Studio will be the one that can prove it can reuse what it learns.

The smartest deal is not the one that pays the most today.
It is the one that leaves both sides stronger tomorrow.

Here is the practical problem.

Many young Studios negotiate only the Revenue Share percentage because it is easy to understand. 30%, 40%, 50% sounds concrete. It feels like the deal.

But the percentage is only one part of the deal.

The real question is:

> What does the Studio own after the project is finished?

There are four possible things a Studio can own:

1. **Cash Value**: money earned from production fee, minimum guarantee, or Revenue Share.
2. **Asset Value**: code, tools, art pipeline, templates, IP rights, reusable systems.
3. **Learning Value**: Data access, test results, postmortem, creative performance, market reasoning.
4. **Relationship Value**: trust, next-project option, stronger scope, better negotiation position.

Weak teams only see Cash Value.
Better teams see Cash Value and Asset Value.
Strong Operators negotiate for all four.

This matters because a failed game can still be valuable if the Studio understands why it failed. A successful game can still be strategically weak if the Studio receives money but learns nothing about UA, Retention, Monetization, Creative Testing, or Live Ops.

For Vietnam, this is one of the key maturity questions.

Vietnam already has production strength. Many teams can build fast. Many teams can iterate quickly. But the high-value knowledge often sits with the Publisher: market selection, ad Creative Testing, metric thresholds, Portfolio timing, legal protection, IP strategy, and scaling decisions.

If the deal keeps all of that invisible, the Studio remains dependent.

The Publisher may think: “We gave them Revenue Share.”

The Studio may think: “We worked hard, but still do not know how to repeat success.”

Both sides are partly right.

That is why deal structure should include a learning loop.

A practical learning loop can be simple:

- Before production: shared hypothesis and kill criteria;
- During production: shared milestone review and risk log;
- Before test: agreed event map and expected player behavior;
- After test: Data review and written postmortem;
- After decision: clear reason for scale, iterate, pause, or kill;
- After project: reusable lessons and next-project recommendation.

This does not require the Publisher to expose every confidential detail.
It does require enough transparency for the Studio to improve.

If I were a Publisher, I would treat learning rights as a way to build a stronger supply network. The more the Studio learns, the less I need to explain from zero next time.

If I were a Studio, I would treat learning rights as part of compensation. Sometimes the learning from a serious Publisher is worth more than a slightly higher Revenue Share from a weak one.

The hard truth:

> A high Revenue Share on a blind deal can still be a bad deal.

For the next 2-4 years, I expect better Vietnamese Studios to become more selective. They will not only ask how much they can earn. They will ask whether the partnership helps them move from production team to operating team.

That is the right direction.

Money pays the month.
Learning compounds the company.

If you want to use this in a real negotiation, ask one final question:

> If this project fails, what exactly will the Studio still own?

If the answer is nothing useful, the deal may be too thin.
If the answer includes learning, assets, and a better next step, the deal is probably doing real work.
