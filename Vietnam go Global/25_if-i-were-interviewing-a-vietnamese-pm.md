# If I Were Interviewing A Vietnamese PM

A PM is not just someone who can keep tasks moving.
In games, a PM must keep the outcome moving.

That distinction matters even more in Vietnam, where many PMs grow out of production environments and may not have had many chances to practice ownership under pressure.

If I were interviewing a Vietnamese PM, I would not begin with “Do you know Jira?”
I would begin with:

- Tell me about the last project that failed.
- What did you do when the team started drifting?
- How did you escalate risk?
- What did you do before the deadline slipped?
- What did you learn that you can reuse now?

The formula is:

> Good PM = ownership + clarity + escalation + outcome thinking

That is what matters.

A good PM should be able to:

- Make ambiguity smaller;
- Tell the truth early;
- Keep the team aligned;
- Know what to push and what to stop;
- Understand the business consequence of delay or confusion.

If I were a Publisher, I would care less about general confidence and more about evidence of this behavior under pressure.

If I were a Vietnamese Founder, I would want a PM who can keep a decision trail, not just a task list.

This is especially important for game work because production noise can hide real risk. A PM who notices drift early can save a project. A PM who stays polite and silent can help kill it.

In the next 2-4 years, I expect PM quality to become one of the main differentiators between Studios that merely ship and Studios that actually learn.

PMs who can think in outcomes, not just outputs, will be much more valuable.

I would also test whether the PM understands power.

Many young PMs think their job is to keep everyone happy. That is understandable, but it is incomplete. In a game team, the PM often sits between people with different incentives.

The Publisher wants speed, learning, and Portfolio fit.
The Founder wants Cashflow, reputation, and relationship protection.
The Developer wants stable scope and enough time.
The Designer wants room to test ideas.
The Artist wants clear visual direction.
The UA or Growth team wants creative angles that can attract traffic.

If the PM only tries to please everyone, the project becomes unclear.

The PM must know how to turn tension into a decision.

That means asking:

- What is the tradeoff?
- Who owns the decision?
- What happens if we do nothing?
- What is the cheapest test?
- What risk must be visible today?
- What can wait until after the next Data point?

This is why writing matters so much.

In cross-border work, spoken agreement is fragile. Accents, cultural politeness, fast calls, implied assumptions, and different industry backgrounds can all create misunderstanding.

A strong Vietnamese PM should use written communication as a management tool.

Before a meeting, send:

- Purpose;
- Questions;
- Decisions needed;
- Risks;
- Context links;
- What the team is currently assuming.

After a meeting, send:

- Summary;
- Decisions;
- Open questions;
- Owners;
- Deadlines;
- Changed scope;
- Risks;
- Next checkpoint.

This is not administrative work.

This is how the PM protects the company.

I would also ask the PM how they use AI.

Not because AI replaces PM judgment, but because a modern PM who does not use AI for thinking support will be slower than necessary.

Good uses:

- Turn meeting transcripts into action lists;
- Compare multiple briefs and find conflicts;
- Draft risk registers;
- Summarize Data observations for non-Data teammates;
- Prepare interview questions;
- Create test checklists;
- Rewrite unclear updates into direct English;
- Build simple decision trees.

Bad uses:

- Generating generic strategy;
- Hiding weak thinking behind polished writing;
- Accepting AI answers without checking context;
- Sending long summaries nobody can use.

If I were interviewing a PM, I would ask them to show me a real example of:

- A project update;
- A risk log;
- A postmortem;
- A meeting summary;
- A scope-cut decision.

Documents reveal how a PM thinks under pressure.

A CV can exaggerate.
Documents are harder to fake.

In 2026-2029, I expect the PM role in Vietnamese game teams to become more important, not less. As AI increases production speed, the bottleneck will move toward clarity, judgment, and coordination.

The PM who only tracks tasks will be replaceable.

The PM who can connect Business, Product, Data, People, and Execution will become a leverage point.

If I were a young Vietnamese PM, I would not try to look senior by using bigger words.

I would become senior by making decisions easier for everyone around me.

I would also ask one behavioral question:

> Tell me about a time you had to send a difficult update in writing.

Why?

Because PMs do not just move tasks.
They carry bad news, unclear news, and tradeoff news.

A good answer shows:

- How they think about truth;
- How they write under pressure;
- Whether they protect relationships while staying direct;
- Whether they can tell the team what actually changed.

I would also ask:

> What is the worst misunderstanding you have had with a Publisher, and how did you fix it?

That question reveals whether the PM can learn from cross-border friction.

If the PM can answer with specifics, I trust them more.
If they answer only with vague confidence, I become cautious.

For a Vietnamese team going global, this role is too important to be treated as admin.

The PM is often the person who turns chaos into a decision trail.

That is why I would hire for evidence, not adjectives.

Show me the recap.
Show me the risk log.
Show me how you corrected the misunderstanding.
That tells me more than a confident introduction.
