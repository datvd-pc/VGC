# What To Control, What To Trust

Working with a Vietnamese team is not about micro-managing everything.
It is about choosing the right control points.

That distinction matters.

If you control too much, you slow the team down and make them passive.
If you trust too much, you lose money, time, and clarity.

The right question is not “How do I control more?”
The right question is “What should I never leave vague?”

The items that usually need control are:

- Scope;
- Milestone;
- Data;
- IP rights;
- Source access;
- Risk log;
- Decision gate;
- Delivery format.

The items that can often be trusted are:

- Execution style;
- Local hiring;
- Internal task flow;
- The team’s own way of solving the work;
- Some details of production rhythm.

The formula is:

> Control = scope + milestone + Data + IP + risk log  
> Trust = execution style + local flow

That means you are not trying to micromanage every move.
You are trying to make sure the critical decision points are explicit.

This is especially important in Vietnam because many teams work fast and adapt well, but may still be learning how to make risks visible early. If the control points are fuzzy, both sides will carry different expectations.

If I were a Publisher, I would define the gates:

- What is done;
- Who reviews;
- What Data must exist;
- What the kill criteria are;
- What rights belong to whom.

If I were a Studio, I would protect execution freedom inside that clarity.

In the next 2-4 years, the best partnerships in Vietnam will not be the most controlling ones. They will be the ones that are clearest about what matters and flexible about how the work is done.

Trust without control is naive.
Control without trust is expensive.

This principle also helps you build better working habits.

If you are too loose, you lose visibility.
If you are too tight, you lose speed.

The right answer is to create explicit control points and leave everything else as flexible as possible.

For example:

- Control the scope;
- Control the milestone;
- Control the Data event map;
- Control the IP and source access;
- Control the risk log;
- Control the definition of done;
- Control the decision moment.

Then trust the team to execute inside that frame.

That is how good partnerships work.

It gives the Publisher confidence.
It gives the Studio room to operate.

If I were a Publisher, I would use control to reduce ambiguity, not to kill initiative.
If I were a Studio, I would accept clear gates if I also get room to move inside them.

That balance is what makes a Vietnam partnership productive instead of exhausting.

The best teams will feel that balance in the workflow itself.

One more point matters here.

Control should be strongest where failure is expensive and trust is weakest.

That usually means:

- Scope;
- Milestone;
- Data event map;
- IP and source access;
- Risk log;
- Decision moment.

Trust should be strongest where the team actually has local advantage.

That often means:

- Execution style;
- Local hiring;
- Internal workflow;
- Task sequencing;
- Creative iteration details.

This is not a philosophical point.
It is an operating rule.

If you control too much, you kill initiative.
If you trust too much, you lose visibility.

The best setup is explicit gates plus local freedom inside the gates.

For a Publisher, that means you do not need to micromanage every move.
You need the right checkpoints.

For a Studio, that means you do not need to ask permission for every detail.
You need to respect the frame and move fast inside it.

That is how a serious partnership feels in practice.

If you want the simplest version:

Control what can break the business;
Trust what local speed can improve.

That is the rule I would use.

It is simple enough to remember in a meeting and strong enough to keep the partnership sane.
