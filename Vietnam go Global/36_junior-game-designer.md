# Junior Game Designer

Junior game Designers often know mechanics before they understand behavior.
That is normal.
It is also why many of them struggle at the start.

A level is not just something to make harder.
It is something that should create:

- Clarity;
- Fairness;
- Near-miss;
- Willingness to retry;
- A reason to return.

That is the real job.

The formula is:

> Good level design = clarity + fairness + near-miss + return reason

If a junior Designer only thinks in difficulty, they will often make the player feel trapped instead of engaged.
If they only think in novelty, they may build something interesting but not usable.

What should a junior Designer learn first?

- FAR;
- APS;
- SR;
- Fail-to-retry;
- Fail-to-quit;
- Near-miss;
- Booster value;
- Reward timing;
- Clarity vs difficulty.

Those are much more useful than random buzzwords.

AI can help them learn faster:

- Review level notes;
- Compare mechanics;
- Summarize player feedback;
- Log level hypotheses;
- Detect patterns in fail Data.

But AI will not replace the core judgment.
The Designer still has to ask:

- Is this level fair?
- Does this failure feel understandable?
- Does the player want to try again?
- Is the reward timed well?

If I were a junior Designer, I would build a habit of writing down hypotheses for every level band.
If I were a lead, I would review the team’s level decisions through Data and player memory, not just taste.

In the next 2-4 years, junior Designers who can read Data and trust curve will stand out fast.

A level is a trust engine, not only a challenge engine.

The first lesson for a Junior Game Designer is this:

> Players do not quit only because a level is hard. They quit when the failure feels unfair, unclear, repetitive, or not worth another try.

That is why difficulty is too simple as a design lens.

A stronger lens is player trust.

The player asks silently:

- Do I understand what happened?
- Do I believe I could do better next time?
- Was the loss my fault or the system’s fault?
- Is the reward worth another attempt?
- Is the game respecting my time?

For puzzle and hybrid puzzle, this is critical.

A level can be mathematically solvable and still feel unfair.
A booster can be useful and still feel forced.
A fail state can drive Monetization and still damage trust.
A near-miss can create motivation or create anger, depending on timing.

Junior Designers need to learn how to read these differences.

A practical level review checklist:

- What is the first thing the player should notice?
- What is the main decision in this level?
- What mistake is the level trying to teach?
- Is the difficulty visible before the failure?
- Does the player have a meaningful option?
- Is the near-miss believable?
- Is the booster placement helpful or manipulative?
- What would make the player retry?
- What would make the player quit?

AI can help turn this into a routine.

Prompt:

> Review this puzzle level concept. Check clarity, fairness, player agency, near-miss, fail-to-retry, fail-to-quit, booster value, and reward timing. Ask questions before giving suggestions.

But the Designer must still play, observe, and compare with Data.

The learning loop should be:

> Hypothesis -> build -> observe -> Data -> lesson -> next level

Not:

> Idea -> build -> opinion -> more opinion

If I were training Junior Designers in Vietnam, I would ask them to write one sentence before every level:

> This level is testing whether the player can...

And one sentence after the level:

> The player failed because...

Those two sentences force design thinking.

In the next 2-4 years, Game Design will become more Data-aware and tool-assisted. But the core job will not disappear.

The Designer still needs taste.
The Designer still needs empathy.
The Designer still needs to understand player trust.

AI can speed up learning.
It cannot feel the player’s frustration for you.

A junior Designer should also learn what not to overdo.

Common mistakes:

- Adding too many mechanics too early;
- Confusing difficulty with quality;
- Copying a reference without understanding the player promise;
- Ignoring fail-to-retry behavior;
- Tuning by taste only.

Good designers build a habit of looking for evidence:

- What did the player notice first?
- Where did the player hesitate?
- Where did the player fail?
- Did they retry?
- Did they understand the point?

That habit is what turns raw interest into real skill.

If I were coaching a junior, I would not ask them to design more.

I would ask them to explain more clearly:

- Why this level exists;
- What this level is testing;
- What metric or observation matters;
- What they would change next.

That is where growth begins.
