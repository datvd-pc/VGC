# The Yes Problem

In Vietnam, “yes” does not always mean alignment.

This is one of the most expensive misunderstandings in cross-border game work.

A team can say yes because they understand.
A team can say yes because they want to be polite.
A team can say yes because they do not want to create friction.
A team can say yes because they think they can figure it out later.

Those are not the same thing.

If you are a foreign partner, this matters a lot. If you think “yes” means full alignment, you may only discover the gap when the deadline is close and the work is already drifting.

That is why the problem is not language alone. It is operating clarity.

The formula is:

> Alignment = repeat-back + written decision + risk escalation

What should happen instead?

Before the meeting:

- Send the purpose;
- Send the questions;
- Send the risks;
- Send the decision needed.

During the meeting:

- Repeat back the decision in your own words;
- Confirm owner;
- Confirm deadline;
- Name the uncertainty;
- Say what can go wrong.

After the meeting:

- Write the summary;
- Confirm the owner;
- Confirm the next step;
- Confirm what is still open.

That written trail matters. It is not bureaucracy. It is how you avoid acting on different interpretations of the same conversation.

For a Vietnamese team, this is a skill worth building early. It will improve trust with foreign partners, reduce rework, and make you look much more professional than silent agreement ever can.

If I were a PM, I would not trust verbal yes alone.
If I were a Studio, I would train the team to say:

- What we understand;
- What we do not understand;
- What we will do first;
- What risk we see;
- What needs confirmation.

In the next 2-4 years, the teams that can say “yes” and still write clearly will be the teams foreign partners trust most.

There is a simple habit that solves a lot of this.

After every important conversation, ask the team to write three lines:

1. What do we think we agreed on?
2. What are we still unsure about?
3. What will we do next?

That one habit can uncover a huge amount of hidden misalignment.

It is also easy to combine with AI.

You can use AI to turn messy meeting notes into a short recap, then ask the team to validate it.
That is much better than assuming alignment just because everyone nodded.

For Vietnam teams, this is a very high-value skill because it makes communication visible.
It reduces the gap between intention and execution.

It also builds confidence with foreign partners because it shows seriousness.

If I were a junior, I would get used to writing these three lines after any important call.
If I were a PM, I would make this a team rule.

That small rule reduces a lot of hidden waste.

It is one of the simplest ways to make a team look and act more professional.

That rule is simple, but it changes the whole tone of the relationship.

It tells the other side that you care about accuracy, not just harmony.

In game work, that is a real strength.

It is a small habit.
It prevents large misunderstandings.

Why does the “yes” problem happen so often?

Because “yes” can mean at least four things:

- I heard you.
- I roughly agree.
- I want to avoid conflict.
- I will do it exactly as you asked.

Those are not the same.

In a cross-border game team, this becomes expensive fast.

The fix is to force the meaning into a written answer:

> Do you mean yes to the direction, yes to the scope, or yes to the deadline?

That question feels simple.
It is powerful.

It makes the response concrete.

Another useful habit is to ask for a short summary in the other person’s words:
