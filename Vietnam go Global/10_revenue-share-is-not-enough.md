# Revenue Share Is Not Enough

Revenue Share sounds like success. For a Vietnamese Studio, it can be. But it is not enough.

A Studio can make real money from a hit and still end up with very little long-term power.
That happens when the Studio gets paid for success but does not keep enough of the learning, the rights, or the operating control.

This is the part many people miss.

A hit creates cash. It does not automatically create a company that can repeat the result.

In Vietnam, that matters even more because a hit can already change a Founder’s life. The cost of living is lower than in many global hubs, so a decent Rev-Share can make a team feel “done enough.” That is rational. It is not weakness. But it changes the incentive.

Some teams will cash out.
Some will become lifestyle Studios.
Some will chase the next hit.
Very few will keep reinvesting into operating maturity.

The formula is simple:

> Revenue Share rewards success. It does not automatically build maturity.

That distinction is critical for Publishers too.

If you are a Publisher, you may think a Rev-Share deal aligns the Studio with you. Sometimes it does. But revenue alone does not tell you whether the Studio will:

- Learn from the Data;
- Keep improving the system;
- Retain key staff;
- Build Live Ops muscle;
- Reuse the learning on the next title.

If they do not, then the deal may have made them richer, but not stronger.

That is why better deals need to think beyond payout.

The real questions are:

- Who owns the IP?
- Who owns the source?
- Who owns the dashboards and post-launch learning?
- Who decides whether to scale or kill?
- What rights does the Studio keep after the launch?
- What can be reused on the next game?

This is also where many Studios in Vietnam overestimate the value of the hit itself.

The truth is:

> A hit reveals product-market fit. It does not reveal company-market fit.

Company-market fit is harder.
It asks whether the team can keep operating after the first success, not just celebrate it.

In the next 2-4 years, I expect this gap to become more visible. AI will keep production cheap. That means more teams will be able to make something launchable. But fewer will know how to turn one good result into a repeatable business.

If I were a Studio, I would ask:

- What capability did we really gain from this deal?
- What part of the system belongs to us?
- What would we know if the Publisher disappeared tomorrow?

If I were a Publisher, I would ask:

- Does this Studio want cash-out, lifestyle, repeat-hit, or operator-building?
- Are we funding a team or funding a system?
- Are we paying for a title, or for a learning partner?

That is why Revenue Share must be seen as a payment model, not a strategy.

It can be part of a good strategy.
It cannot be the whole strategy.

If you are working with Vietnam Studios, the deal is not only about how money flows. It is about whether the learning stays.

There is also a Founder psychology layer here.

When money finally arrives, many people instinctively want to stop bleeding. That makes sense. They have been under pressure for a long time. They may finally be able to breathe.

But breathing is not the same as building.

If the team uses the success only to recover, it may never build the next layer of capability.
That is why some teams become comfortable too early.

For a Publisher, this matters because a profitable Studio may still be a weak long-term partner if it does not know how to turn success into an operating system.

For a Founder, this matters because the right response to a hit is not only celebration. It is reinvestment:

- Into Data;
- Into Live Ops;
- Into better decision-making;
- Into a stronger team;
- Into legal and product discipline.

That is how a win becomes a platform.

The market is not short of moments. It is short of compounding.

Revenue Share can create a good moment.
It cannot create compounding by itself.

That is why it is not enough.

The deeper issue is ownership of the future.

If you only share Revenue Share, you are sharing one outcome.
If you share Data, you are sharing understanding.
If you share source access, you are sharing future reuse.
If you share postmortems, you are sharing learning.
If you share IP, you are sharing long-term value.

That is why the better deal is not only about revenue. It is about what the Studio can do better next time.

If I were a Studio, I would ask six questions before celebrating any Revenue Share deal:

- What can we see during the test?
- What can we reuse after the test?
- What can we learn even if the game fails?
- What rights do we keep if the game succeeds?
- What happens if the Publisher pauses the project?
- What happens if the team needs to pivot?

If I were a Publisher, I would ask the mirror questions:

- Are we giving enough visibility for the Studio to improve?
- Are we protecting our investment without starving the Studio of learning?
- Are we creating a one-off vendor, or a stronger partner?

This is where many deals are either great or weak.

They are not weak because the percentage is low.
They are weak because the structure does not create compounding.

That is the point.

A good Revenue Share deal should help both sides become better at the next deal.

If it does not, it is only a temporary financial arrangement.

If you want a simple test, ask:

> Does this deal make the next project easier to build, easier to understand, or easier to scale?

If the answer is no, then the deal is probably too thin.
