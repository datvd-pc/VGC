# After The Meeting, Send Summary, Owners, Next Steps

If there is no summary, the meeting may as well not have happened.

That is especially true when teams work across cultures, time zones, or different assumptions.

A good meeting does not end when people leave the call.
It ends when the decisions are written down.

The formula is:

> Meeting output = summary + owner + deadline + risk + next step

After the meeting, make sure the recap includes:

- What was decided;
- Who owns each item;
- When it is due;
- What risk remains;
- What is still open;
- What the next step is.

Do that fast.
Ideally within the same day.

This is not about bureaucracy. It is about reducing drift.

Without written recap, people will remember different things.
Some will remember the action.
Some will remember the tone.
Some will remember only the part they cared about.
Then the work starts to split.

AI is very useful here.

It can turn notes into:

- A clean summary;
- An email confirm;
- A list of action items;
- A short risk note.

But the person still needs to check the result and make sure it is true.

If I were a PM, I would always send the summary.
If I were a Studio lead, I would make written recap a habit.
If I were a junior, I would learn that good follow-up is part of professionalism.

In the next 2-4 years, people who write strong follow-up will build more trust than people who only speak well in the room.

The meeting is not over until the memory is written.

Here is the simple template I would teach every junior PM, Game Designer, and team lead:

> Hi team, here is my understanding from today’s discussion. Please correct me if I missed anything.  
>  
> Summary:  
> Decisions:  
> Owners:  
> Deadlines:  
> Open Questions:  
> Risks:  
> Next Step:

The first sentence matters.

“Please correct me if I missed anything” gives the other side an easy way to fix misunderstanding without creating conflict.

This is important when working across cultures.

A Vietnamese teammate may avoid direct disagreement in the call. A Western Partner may assume silence means agreement. A Founder may remember only the business decision. A Developer may remember only the technical constraint. A Designer may remember only the feature direction.

The written recap turns all of that into one shared memory.

Bad recap:

> We discussed the build. Team will update soon.

This is almost useless.

Better recap:

> Summary: Build 0.3 will focus on core loop readability.  
> Decision: meta progression is postponed until after the next playtest.  
> Owner: Dev team owns playable build, Designer owns onboarding revision, PM owns event checklist.  
> Deadline: build by Friday 5 PM Vietnam time.  
> Risk: current tutorial may still be too long.  
> Open Question: confirm whether we need video capture for Publisher review.  
> Next Step: send build, gameplay video, and event checklist.

That recap reduces drift immediately.

AI can help create the first draft.

Prompt:

> Convert these notes into a concise meeting recap. Include Summary, Decisions, Owners, Deadlines, Open Questions, Risks, and Next Step. Use clear English. Do not add information that is not in the notes.

Then check:

- Did AI invent anything?
- Are owners named clearly?
- Are deadlines specific?
- Are risks visible?
- Is the next step obvious?
- Can a person who missed the meeting understand what happened?

This habit also helps career growth.

People trust the person who creates clarity after discussion.

If you are junior, sending strong follow-up is one of the simplest ways to be noticed.
If you are a PM, it is part of your core job.
If you are a Founder, it protects the company from memory-based management.
If you are a Publisher PM, it reduces the chance that your Vietnam partner misunderstands the actual decision.

In 2026-2029, I expect this skill to matter more because work will move faster. AI will accelerate output. Remote collaboration will stay normal. Teams will handle more tools, more Data, more versions, and more decisions.

The bottleneck will not only be making things.

The bottleneck will be keeping everyone aligned while making things.

That is why the recap is not clerical work.

It is operating leverage.

The same applies to internal team meetings, not only Publisher calls.

If your Dev, Designer, and PM leave the room with different memories, the team will pay later.

So I would use the same format for every important discussion:

> Summary  
> Decision  
> Owner  
> Deadline  
> Risk  
> Next step

That format is simple enough for juniors, and strong enough for leads.

It also creates a paper trail for learning.

When the next problem happens, you can check what was actually decided instead of arguing from memory.

That alone is worth a lot.
