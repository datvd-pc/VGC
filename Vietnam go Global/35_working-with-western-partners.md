# Working With Western Partners

Working with Western Partners is not mostly about English.
It is about explicit alignment.

That is a very different skill.

Many Vietnamese teams understand enough English to get through a meeting, but still misunderstand the real intent. The problem is usually not vocabulary. It is assumptions, tempo, and the habit of leaving things implicit.

Western Partners usually expect:

- Clear purpose before the meeting;
- Clear questions during the meeting;
- Clear written recap after the meeting;
- Clear ownership and deadline on paper.

The formula is:

> Western working = explicit prep + explicit confirm + explicit trail

That means:

Before the meeting:

- Send the purpose;
- Send the questions;
- Send the risks;
- Send what you need decided.

During the meeting:

- Repeat back the decision;
- Clarify what is still uncertain;
- Confirm owner and deadline.

After the meeting:

- Send a summary;
- Send an email confirm;
- Leave a written trail.

That trail matters because people remember different things.

If you do not write it down, the work will split.
If you do not repeat it back, you may have different meanings for the same “yes.”

AI can help a lot here:

- Drafting the agenda;
- Rewriting notes into a cleaner recap;
- Translating a messy thought into a short email;
- Summarizing long meetings.

For a Vietnamese team, this is not just about foreign partners.
It is about building professionalism that can work across cultures.

If I were a PM, I would insist on written confirmation.
If I were a Studio, I would train the team to expect it.

In the next 2-4 years, teams that can work clearly with Western Partners will be much easier to trust and much easier to scale.

The most common misunderstanding is agreement.

In many Vietnamese teams, people may say “yes” to show that they heard the message, respect the speaker, or accept the direction in principle.

In many Western business settings, “yes” is often understood as:

> I agree, I understand, I can deliver, and I accept the responsibility.

Those are not the same.

That gap creates real damage.

A Vietnamese team may leave the meeting thinking:

> We will try.

The Western Partner may leave thinking:

> They committed.

Two weeks later, both sides are frustrated.

The fix is to separate the meanings:

- “I understand.”
- “I agree.”
- “I can deliver.”
- “I need to check.”
- “I see a risk.”
- “I disagree because...”

These sentences are simple, but they reduce confusion.

Before every important decision, I would ask:

> Are we confirming direction, or are we confirming commitment?

That one question can save a project.

Vietnamese teams should also get comfortable with respectful pushback.

Pushback does not mean fighting.
Pushback means making tradeoffs visible.

Useful phrases:

> We can do this, but it will affect the test date.  
> I agree with the goal, but I see a risk in the current scope.  
> We need to confirm whether this is must-have or nice-to-have.  
> If the priority is speed, I recommend cutting this part.  
> I may be missing context, but here is my concern.

That kind of language is professional.

For Western Partners, the lesson is also clear:

Do not assume silence means alignment.
Do not assume politeness means confidence.
Do not assume fast execution means deep understanding.

Ask the Vietnamese team to write back:

- What they understood;
- What they will do;
- What risk they see;
- What they need confirmed;
- What they will not do in this scope.

This is not because Vietnamese teams are weak.
It is because cross-cultural work needs explicit operating habits.

In the next 2-4 years, more foreign Publishers will look at Vietnam. The ones who succeed will not be the ones who only search for cheaper production. They will be the ones who understand how to create clarity, trust, and learning with local teams.

The best partnership is not where both sides talk more.

It is where both sides misunderstand less.

There is also a practical communication standard that helps a lot.

Use “confirm / clarify / propose” instead of only “yes / no.”

Examples:

- Confirm: “I understand the scope.”
- Clarify: “I want to check the deadline.”
- Propose: “I suggest we cut this part for now.”

These phrases are direct and respectful.
They reduce the chance of hidden agreement.

For Vietnamese teams, that is especially useful because politeness can sometimes hide uncertainty.

For Western Partners, it is also useful because they get a clearer signal.

The best cross-border teams do not just speak English.
They build a shared operating language.

That language says:

- Here is the goal;
- Here is the risk;
- Here is the tradeoff;
- Here is the decision;
- Here is the recap.

That is enough to make trust grow.
